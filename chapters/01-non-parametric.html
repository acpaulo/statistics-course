Non-parametric statistics

What can we do when the assumptions behind familiar parametric methods are not appropriate? Learn to choose, perform and interpret rank-based tests.

1 · Biological question

What if the assumptions are not convincing?

Many familiar statistical methods use a model for the data. The classical Student two-sample t-test assumes normally distributed observations within each group and equal population variances. Welch’s t-test does not require equal variances, but still relies on a model for the sampling distribution of the difference in means.

Biological measurements may be strongly skewed, contain extreme observations, or come from small samples. Rank-based non-parametric methods provide alternatives that use the ordering of observations rather than their raw numerical values.

Do not choose a test mechanically from a normality-test result. Consider the design, independence, sample size, distribution shape, outliers and the biological quantity you want to compare.

2 · Choosing an approach

What changes when we use ranks?

A t-test uses the numerical values to compare means. The Mann–Whitney test pools the observations, orders them, and compares their ranks between groups.

DesignParametric exampleRank-based alternative
Two independent groupsWelch t-testMann–Whitney U / Wilcoxon rank-sum
Two paired measurementsPaired t-testWilcoxon signed-rank
3+ independent groupsANOVAKruskal–Wallis
3+ related conditionsRepeated-measures ANOVAFriedman test
Terminology: Mann–Whitney U and Wilcoxon rank-sum are two names for the same independent-groups procedure. Wilcoxon signed-rank is a different test for paired data.

Rank-based tests are not assumption-free. The usual Mann–Whitney test still requires independent observations. It tests whether the population distributions differ; interpreting it as a median/location comparison is most appropriate when distribution shapes and spreads are similar.

3 · Hypotheses, test statistic and p-value

What does a p-value mean?

For a two-sided comparison of two independent groups, a common formulation is:

H0: the two population distributions are identical
H1: the two population distributions differ

The test statistic summarises how incompatible the observed data are with H0. Under H0, it follows a null distribution. The p-value is the probability, assuming H0 is true, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one.

Alpha (α) is selected before the analysis, often 0.05. It defines the long-run Type I error rate of the test procedure under its assumptions. The p-value is calculated from the observed data. If p ≤ α, reject H0; otherwise, do not reject H0.

A p-value is not the probability that H0 is true. It is not the probability that the result happened “by chance”. Statistical significance does not measure effect magnitude or biological importance.

Which distribution is used for a non-parametric test?

It is essential to distinguish the distribution of the original observations from the null distribution of the test statistic. A rank-based test does not require the original measurements to be normally distributed. However, to calculate a p-value, we still need to know how the test statistic would behave if the null hypothesis were true.

Three different ideas:
1. Data distribution: the shape of the measured values (which may be skewed).
2. Rank distribution: the ranks assigned after pooling and ordering the observations.
3. Null distribution of the test statistic: the distribution of U values expected under H0.

Mann–Whitney U: exact distribution for small samples

Under the null hypothesis that the two groups are sampled from the same continuous distribution, group labels are exchangeable. For fixed sample sizes, we can consider all possible ways of allocating the pooled ranks to the two groups. This gives the exact null distribution of U.

For example, with three observations in each group, there are:

Number of allocations = (6 choose 3) = 20

Each allocation produces a rank sum and a U statistic. The exact p-value is the proportion of allocations that produce a statistic at least as extreme as the observed statistic (using the chosen one- or two-sided definition).

Normal approximation for larger samples

For larger samples, the null distribution of U is often approximated by a normal distribution. Without ties:

E(U) = n1n2/2
Var(U) = n1n2(n1 + n2 + 1)/12

We can standardise U to obtain a Z statistic:

Z = [U − E(U)] / √Var(U)

A continuity correction may be applied. When tied observations occur, the variance must be adjusted for ties. Software generally handles these details, but students should understand why a normal distribution may appear in the output of a non-parametric test.

Key distinction: the normal distribution here is an approximation to the null distribution of the test statistic. It is not an assumption that the original biological measurements are normally distributed.

And the Wilcoxon signed-rank test?

For paired data, calculate the within-pair differences, remove zero differences as appropriate, rank the absolute differences, and retain their signs. Under the usual null model, the paired-difference distribution is assumed to be symmetric around zero for the signed-rank test to support a location-shift interpretation. For small samples, an exact sign/permutation distribution can be used; for larger samples, a normal approximation is commonly used. Thus, the signed-rank test also has a null distribution for its statistic, even though the original differences are not required to be normally distributed.

4 · Mann–Whitney U

Two independent groups

Use Mann–Whitney U when the outcome is at least ordinal and observations are independent. It compares the group distributions. If their shapes and spreads are similar, it is often interpreted as a location difference; it is not generally just a test of medians.

Worked example: rank the pooled observations

GroupValuesRanksRank sum
Control0.8, 1.0, 1.11, 2, 36
Antibiotic1.3, 1.6, 2.04, 5, 615

For control, n₁ = 3 and rank sum R₁ = 6:

U₁ = R₁ − n₁(n₁ + 1)/2 = 6 − 6 = 0

U₂ = n₁n₂ − U₁ = 9. The smaller U is 0. With only three observations per group, the exact two-sided p-value is 0.10—even though the values are completely separated. This illustrates the limited resolution of very small samples.

Interpretation: the observed separation is suggestive, but the exact two-sided test does not reach α = 0.05. A non-significant result is not proof that the groups are identical.

Effect size

Cliff’s delta measures stochastic separation between groups A and B:

δ = P(A > B) − P(A < B)

It ranges from −1 to +1. The sign depends on group order. Report group summaries, an effect size and uncertainty where available, alongside the p-value.

7 · Take-home messages

What should you remember?

  • Start with the biological question and experimental design.
  • Non-parametric tests are not assumption-free.
  • Mann–Whitney U is for two independent groups; Wilcoxon signed-rank is for paired observations.
  • State H₀ and H₁, identify the test statistic, interpret p relative to α, and report an effect size.
  • Statistical evidence, effect magnitude and biological importance are distinct.
